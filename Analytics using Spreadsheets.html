Base case

1. Base-Case Analysis

  • Base case serves as a point of comparison.
  • Current policy or common practice.
  • Most likely scenario, best or worst case scenario.
  • Answers questions such as: If we follow last year’s plan, how much profit should we expect next year?
  • How many items do we expect to sell next week (assuming we are in Q1)?

What-if

2. What-If Analysis

Varying a Parameter

  • Asking what-if given information were different.
  • Tests numerical assumptions of model.
  • Example: How much will profit change if our product costs turn out to be 10% higher or 10% lower than we have assumed?

Varying a Decision

  • Explores outcomes we can influence (by varying inputs we can control).
  • Leads to better decisions.
  • Example: How much will profit change if we spend an extra $1000 on advertising in the first quarter?

Varying the Model Structure

  • Tests key structural assumptions in model.
  • More complex than changes to parameters or decision variables.
  • Example: How does profit change if we change our non-linear model of sales and advertising to a linear one?
  • Unit Sales = 3000 + 0.1 × (Advertising Expenses × Seasonal Factor)
  • What is the impact on profit if the Advertising Expenditure in the first quarter is $11,000? Compare it to the base case setting.

Parametric Sensitivity

  • Tabulates output based over a range of inputs.
    • Versus just one (e.g., what-if analysis).
  • Steps:
    • A. For a single numerical result: Analytic Solver Platform → Analysis → Sensitivity:
      • Open Function Arguments Window
    • B. For a table of graph plotting, e.g., profit as a function of unit cost: Analytic Solver Platform → Analysis → Sensitivity → Parameter Analysis:
      • Opens sensitivity report window

Tornado Charts

  • Shows sensitivity to several inputs (versus one or two in parametric sensitivity).
  • Reveals which inputs have major vs. minor impacts.
  • Limitations:
    • Potential pitfalls in assuming every parameter varies by the same percentage as the base case.
    • Sensitivity implied can be misleading if output related to input in a nonlinear fashion.
    • In general, we must be cautious about drawing conclusions about the sensitivity of a model outside the range of the parameters tested in a tornado chart.

Break-Even, Optimization & Risk

3. Break-Even Analysis

  • Analyzes where a particular point of interest occurs.
  • At what cost will the firm break even?

4. Optimization Analysis

  • Finds set of decision variables that achieves best possible value of an output.
  • Answers questions such as:
    • How should we allocate our budget to maximize profit?
    • How much inventory should we stock of each type of product, given constraints on shelf size and budget?
  • Chapters 6–10 develop techniques to answer these and a host of related questions for a variety of spreadsheet models.

5. Simulation and Risk Analysis

  • Analyzes the effect of uncertainty on the output.
  • Analytic Solver Platform (which includes Monte Carlo simulation capability) is an important tool.
  • Chapters 14–15 develop techniques to handle such problems.

Working with data

Data: Structure, Types & Exploration

Dataset Structure

  • Spreadsheet databases are two-dimensional files (versus more complex relational databases).
  • Consist of:
    • Rows = records (“cases” or “instances”).
    • Columns = fields (“variables”, “descriptors”, “predictors”).
  • Most databases contain a data dictionary documenting fields in detail.

SALESSales volume in cases

Types of Data

  • Categorical
    • 1. Nominal — categories by specified characteristics; no quantitative relationship between them. Ex: customer location (America, Europe, Asia).
    • 2. Ordinal — ranked or ordered by some relationship; no fixed unit of measurement. Ex: survey responses (poor, average, good, very good, excellent).
  • Numeric
    • 3. Interval — constant differences between observations, but no true zero, so ratios are not meaningful. Ex: temperature readings.
    • 4. Ratio — continuous values with a natural zero point; ratios are meaningful. Ex: monthly sales of two regions.

Data Exploration

  • Databases are highly structured for storage but do not automatically reveal patterns and insights.
  • We explore databases in a five-step process:
  1. Understand the data.
  2. Organize and subset the data.
  3. Examine individual variables graphically.
  4. Calculate summary measures for individual variables.
  5. Examine relationships among variables graphically.
  6. Examine relationships among variables numerically.

In practice

Data Exploration in Practice

1. Understand the Data

  • Be skeptical of data, and ask:
    • How are fields defined?
    • What types of data are represented?
    • What units are the data in?
  • Job Applicants database:
    • SEX and AGE are unambiguous.
    • What about CITZ CODE? Important to know how the variable was coded.

2. Organize & Subset the Database

  • Two essential tools: Sort and Filter.
  • Open Applicants database:
  1. How does work experience vary among applicants in successive rounds?
  2. What are the characteristics of applicants from Nonprofit organizations?
  3. What are the ages of the oldest applicants?
  4. Isolate applicants who worked in either Nonprofit or Government sectors.
  5. … and had GMAT scores above 700.

3. Examine Individual Variables Graphically

  • For numerical variables, know the range of records from lowest to highest, and where most outcomes lie.
    • Typical values for JOB MONTHS and the range from lowest to highest?
  • For categorical variables:
    • What are the most common decisions in the Applicants database?

4. Calculate Summary Measures

  • The most common summary measure of a numerical value is average or mean.
  • Others: median; minimum; maximum.
  • Applicants database:
    • How many applicants have ages less than 20?
    • What percentage were given the DENY decision?
    • Average age of applicants from the Nonprofit sector?

5. Examine Relationships Graphically

  • Relationships among variables are often more important than the properties of one variable.
  • Graphical methods can track relationships.
  • Applicants database:
    • How long have older applicants held their current job?
    • How does the variability of GMAT scores compare across rounds?

6. Examine Relationships Numerically

  • Numerical methods can also track relationships.
  • Applicants database:
    • How does the average GMAT score vary by round and by the industry in which the application is filed?
